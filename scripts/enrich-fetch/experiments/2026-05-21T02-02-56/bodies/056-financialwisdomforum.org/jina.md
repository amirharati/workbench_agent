[https://milliondollarjourney.com/intera ... canada.htm](https://milliondollarjourney.com/interactive-brokers-review-canada.htm)

 "there is a very important question mark when it comes to using any US-based online brokerage as a Canadian resident. This question mark comes in the form of the interpretation of US Tax Law. It’s not that Interactive Brokers is in and of itself unsafe for Canadians to use from a tax standpoint, it’s that the USA has some unique tax rules when it comes to inheritance tax.

 It gets complicated in a hurry, but the basic idea is that the USA taxes “US In Situs Assets” at the time a person passes away. The estate of any Canadian resident who owns over $60,000 worth of assets at the time of their death must file a tax return in the USA. IB is obviously located in the USA, so the question quickly becomes – do investments in my Interactive Brokers account fall under USA inheritance tax law, are they considered “US In Situs Assets”?

 The answer is… *drumroll*… no one really knows.

 It appears to boil down to if your investments are considered “domiciled” in the USA. That phrase is being debated by people smarter than myself each year in the USA. I know that my go-to resource for international personal finance is author Andrew Hallam, and he does not use IB for this exact reason.

 Now if you’re a USA resident, or a USA Citizen, then you’re going to have to deal with US estate taxes anyway – so this drawback to going with IB is not really relevant. If you live in Canada however, I just don’t think saving a couple of bucks on non-ETF transactions are worth it.

 So while your investments are likely as safe as possible from hackers or fraud, it’s perhaps the US taxman that you have to be most careful of...

 It has yet to be definitively stated anywhere that I’m aware of that “domiciled” refers to only where the ETFs are listed, and not the company you are using to broker the purhcasing and holding of the assets. Hopefully it’s just where the ETF is based, but I personally know several millionaires who refuse to hold their assets in IB because of this hazy legal quandry."

 If you had an account with IBKR in the US, that might increase your susceptibility to US estate tax. But I doubt that it will If you have an account with IBKR Canada. From the IBKR Canada website:

 "Interactive Brokers Canada Inc. is a member of the Investment Industry Regulatory Organization of Canada (IIROC) and Member - Canadian Investor Protection Fund..Our registered office is located at 1800 McGill College Avenue, Suite 2106, Montreal, Quebec, H3A 3J6, Canada"

 However, since my tax/legal background could very generously be described as modest, I may be wrong.

I believe that any U.S. stocks held in this account will be considered Situs Assets.

 However, do Canadians resident in Canada not benefit from the agreement with the U.S. whereby you have an exemption from the estate taxes if your total assets are less than USD 12 million (although you might legally still have to file the return, I'm not sure)? Therefore you willl need to be very wealthy for estate taxes to kick in. Note that the 12 million could be reduced at any time, it was that long ago it was only 5.4M and then was doubled by the government at that time.

 I am Canadian non-resident, so i do not benefit from this agreement with the U.S.

 In his book "Millionaire Expat: How To Build Wealth Living Overseas" Andrew states there is some risk that shares held in IB will be subject to estate tax, but i am uncertain what drives the uncertainty.

 I have heard from others that if you had a U.S. Schwabs account, they would not release the assets in the account until they have proof that the U.S. Estate Tax return has been filed; i am uncertain if IB has such a requirement.

 For safety, i would assume that U.S. shares held in any account will be U.S. Situs Assets. If you have total assets greater than the exemption amount, then you should consider holding such shares through a Canadian ETF (not a US Situs Asset), or possibly a trust if that works for you.

 Based on the nature of your question, you might already know more than me. I am very interest in what you learn as I also have found there is uncertainty in this area.

 Additionally, do you know which U.S. bonds are not U.S. Situs Assets? I have had trouble finding the answer to this as well (not all U.S. bonds are).

 My hope is i die some slow (not painful) death, that allows me to recorganize my U.S. Situs Assets before i die (eg. sell them and move the cash to Canada). My biggest fear is a quick death, and my estate paying 40% tax. Death and taxes are guaranteed, but i want to minimize those taxes before my death.

 FYI, I believe the estate tax does not offset other taxes either. So your estate can be subject to both the estate taxes as well as taxes on unrealized capital gains.

The tax boffins at [Manulife](https://www.manulifeim.com/retail/ca/en/viewpoints/tax-planning/us-estate-tax-exposure-for-canadian-residents-who-are-not-us-citizens) say:

> A U.S. estate tax return must be filed with the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) if a deceased Canadian resident (who’s not a U.S. citizen) owned U.S. assets with a fair market value greater than US$60,000 at death. It’s important to note that there’s a requirement to file a U.S. estate tax return even if the application of the Canada-U.S. tax treaty results in no taxes owing. A Canadian resident must file a non-resident U.S. estate tax return (Form 706-NA) to claim their treaty position (i.e., the unified credit) and to make sure their beneficiaries receive a stepped-up basis to fair market value at the time of the decedent’s death. If the Form 706-NA isn’t filed, then there’s a zero basis in the asset in the hands of the beneficiaries, which results in double tax when they dispose of it or pass away.

[Here](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-706-na) is Form 706-NA and its instructions. Looks much simpler than other IRS forms and should not be a big problem for an accountant or other tax pro doing the estate's returns.* NOTE: the instructions say Canadians with total estates worth less than US$1.2 million are due a "small estate exemption."

 * Alas, the tax prep is only half the battle. The big problem ISTM is that the IRS is in such shambles that getting the return processed will likely take months and quite possibly more than a year. Of course, it could go the other way. Overworked IRS folk might simply rubber stamp returns from average estates as soon as they arrive.

These guys are not a tax or accounting firm but have planners on staff and work with really good lawyers and accountants in both Canada and the US. Their Canadian hub is Calgary and US hubs in AZ and FLA which may be of interest to some of those who have commented on this thread. [https://cardinalpointwealth.com/2022/04 ... -property/](https://cardinalpointwealth.com/2022/04/29/cross-border-wealth-management-canadian-residents-with-u-s-real-property/)

 2. The following list was prepared by a US law firm who specialise in non-resident Income, Estate and Gift Tax planning. It was prepared in 2021 so is relatively current. Many of the categories would not be relevant to most of us here but the first few will be common.

**HEALTH WARNING: For interest only, do not rely on this list for any actions you are contemplating.**

 US-SITUS PROPERTY FOR ESTATE TAX PURPOSES DOES INCLUDE:

 • Real property located in the US

 • Tangible personal property located in the US

 • Stock in a US corporation

 • Global Depository Receipts representing stock in a US corporations

 • Debt obligation of US resident, corporation or governmental unit (but not a portfolio interest promissory note)

 • Cash surrender value of domestic life insurance policy

 • Domestic annuity contract

 • Currency in a US safe deposit box

 • Condominium located in the US

 • Long-term lease of US real property

 • Long-term lease of tangible personal property located in the US

 • Intellectual property interest issued by the US government and/or enforceable against a US person

 • Pension, IRA, stock option enforceable against the US issuer or fiduciary

 • US debt obligation (other than a bank deposit) issued before July 18, 1984

 • Mortgage enforceable against a US resident or corporation*

 • Rights in crops, timber, water and minerals connected to US real property

 • Interests in estates and trusts to the extent of US-situs property

 • Interests in proprietorships to the extent of US-situs property

 • Licenses enforceable against a US resident, corporation or governmental unit

 US-SITUS PROPERTY FOR ESTATE TAX PURPOSES DOES NOT INCLUDE:

 • Real property located outside the US

 • Real property located in a US possession

 • Tangible personal property located outside of the US

 • Art on loan to a museum in the US

 • Debt obligation of a non-US person, corporation or governmental unit

 • Portfolio interest promissory note of a US person

 • Stock in a foreign corporation

 • Cash deposit in a US bank (checking, savings)*

 • Cash deposit with a foreign branch of a US bank*

 • Cash deposit with a domestic branch of a foreign bank*

 * Unless effectively connected with the holder’s US trade or business
